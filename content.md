## Be aware

Written in our own words. The source line is not on the page. Liz asked to leave it off the bottom. Do not put it back unless she asks for an About page.

### The trip has to match the people
A well-known trail can still be too far or too steep for your group. Before you go, read how long it is, how much climbing it has, and how many hours it usually takes. If that is too much, choose a smaller trip.

### Town weather can lie
The street can look sunny while the trail is already cold and wet. Read a forecast for the mountain, not only for town. Take a layer you can put on, and a jacket that blocks rain. A very hot day is a problem too.

### Snow can linger up high
The valley can be green while the upper trail still has snow or ice. If your shoes or your skills do not match that, head back down.

### Some slopes can slide
Avalanche terrain is not only for skiers. Some walking routes cross it. In winter and spring, read the Avalanche Canada forecast before you start. Help can be slow to arrive there, because the team has to judge the snow first. Bring more clothes, food, and water than a short walk seems to need.

### Cold water and rising creeks
Howe Sound, creeks, and alpine lakes stay cold even when the air feels warm. A fall in can take your breath. On a boat, kayak, or board, wear the life jacket. A creek that was easy in the morning can be too high on the way back. Fast water, or water that is climbing, means you do not cross.

### You are in their home
Bears and cougars use these trails. Talk while you walk, especially on a bend you cannot see around and beside a creek. Leave them space. Pack food and garbage away. Do not step closer for a photo. On a bike or a run, slow down where the view is blocked, and keep a dog beside you. Spray only helps if you already know how it works.

### Your phone is a weak plan
Plenty of valleys and ridges have no signal. Save the battery for a real call, and bring a separate light. Download a map that works with no service before you leave. If a call goes through, it is 9-1-1. This corridor does not have its own rescue line. Far from cell towers, a satellite messenger is what can still reach someone.

### The road out can fail
A forest road can be rough, blocked by a gate, or buried in snow. Do not bet on the car getting you out once it is dark.

### Dark arrives on its own clock
Set a clock time to turn around, and turn around then. Carry a headlamp. People often need help because they are still out when the light is gone.

## What to do if…

Simple tips, in our own words.

Photos, published 2026-11-18 in commit a8dbb30. The four boxes are the same height.
- After the lost and hurt cards: Blackcomb Powder on Blackcomb Mountain by Tucker Sherman, CC BY 3.0. Resized.
- At the bottom: the old Sea-to-Sky Highway and Howe Sound background, now a circle. That file has no photographer name.

### If you are lost
- Stop. Do not keep walking.
- Stay with your group.
- Stay in that spot unless it is unsafe.
- Call 9-1-1. Ask for police. Say you need Search and Rescue.
- Give your location from the Compass or Maps app on your phone.
- Put on warm clothes before dark.
- Blow a whistle 3 times.
- Do not walk downhill through the bush. Many Sea-to-Sky slopes end at cliffs.

### If someone is hurt
- Make sure you are safe first.
- Call 9-1-1 right away. Ask for police. They activate Search and Rescue.
- Tell the operator: where you are (GPS if you have it), how many people, what happened, the injuries, the weather, and your phone number.
- Stop life-threatening bleeding. Keep the person warm and dry.
- Stay with them. Help can take a long time. Be ready to wait, and have a way to signal a helicopter, such as a bright tarp.
- Send someone to a place with cell service only if 9-1-1 cannot connect.

### If there is an avalanche
- Shout "avalanche."
- Try to move to the side.
- Try to stay on top of the snow.
- Put a hand over your mouth so you can breathe.
- Search only if your group has practiced and has the tools.
- If you have not practiced, stay off the snow pile.
- Call 9-1-1 from a safe spot.

### If you are in the water
- The water here is very cold.
- Keep your face up and breathe slowly.
- A life jacket holds you up.
- Get out as soon as you can.
- Swim only if the shore or the boat is close.
- Do not jump in after someone.
- Reach out, or throw something they can grab.
- Call 9-1-1.

## If your phone has no service

The page is no-service.html. Words taken from Be aware, Tips, and the old if-lost and if-injured print sheets.

Plenty of valleys and ridges have no signal. This corridor does not have its own rescue line.

Before you leave:
- Download a map that works with no service before you leave.
- Save the battery for a real call, and bring a separate light.
- Far from cell towers, a satellite messenger is what can still reach someone.

If you need help and there is no signal:
- Try 9-1-1 anyway. Emergency calls can use any available tower.
- If a call goes through, it is 9-1-1.
- Use a satellite messenger SOS.
- Move only to a nearby ridge, clearing, or known trail if you are sure.
- Keep one person with the injured or tired. Do not split the group far apart.
- Send someone to a place with cell service only if 9-1-1 cannot connect.

### Why this site exists

Hi, I’m Liz. I made this website as a school capstone project here in the Sea-to-Sky area of British Columbia.

I started it because a page like this matters. In 2026, I kept hearing about people who went into the backcountry unprepared. Some of them were very lucky. The local rescue teams were busy.

One weekend in June 2026, Sea to Sky RCMP and Squamish Search and Rescue were tasked to five calls in the Squamish area. Four of those calls came in seven hours. By early August 2026, Pemberton Search and Rescue had already reached its 50th call of the year, 40 percent more than at the same time the year before.

I wanted a simple place, in plain words, that helps people get ready before they go, and know what to do if something goes wrong.

### What you will find here

- Before you leave: a trip plan to fill in and leave with someone you trust.
- Pack lists: hiking, biking, climbing, and winter.
- Be aware: things to watch for on a Sea-to-Sky trip.
- Tips: short safety tips.
- What to do if: if you are lost, if someone is hurt, if there is an avalanche, or if you are in the water.
- No cell service: what to do when a phone has no signal.
- Emergency numbers: who to call.
- Common mistakes: what to avoid.

### Where the information comes from

The sentences on this site are ours. The facts were checked against public advice from Squamish Search and Rescue, Sea to Sky RCMP, AdventureSmart, and the Squamish-Lillooet Regional District.

The pack lists come from lists already used in this project. The emergency numbers were checked against official sources.

This is not an official page of those organizations.
