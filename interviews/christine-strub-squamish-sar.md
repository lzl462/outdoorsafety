## Your Role and Experience

**1. Could you tell me a little about your role in Search and Rescue and what motivated you to get involved?**

My name is Christine Strub and I am with Squamish Search and Rescue Member (SSAR) since April 2011. I am an active member of the Ground Search and Rescue (GSAR) team, the Rope Rescue team, the Medical team and the Community Outreach team (Squamish SAR Prevention).

**2. Is there a rescue or experience that stands out to you that you would feel comfortable sharing? What did you learn from it?**

Every response is different and requires full attention. They key pieces are always to keep the team safe and assist the subject as efficiently and safely as possible.

While we all play in the outdoors, it is very clear that things can go wrong and this is why SAR teams exist and are there to help.

Outdoor users need to understand that it can take a moment to access them, especially if they are in a remote area and maybe the weather or availability does not grant access to a helicopter (likely a shorter extraction time but not guaranteed).

This is why preparedness is so important! It keeps you and your patient comfortable and WARM until we get to you!

In short to answer your question: there is no particular rescue that stands out as they are all very unique and always involve people in difficult situations. Preparedness is the most important and actually applicable to many life situations.

## Common Mistakes and Prevention

**3. What are the most common mistakes people make when exploring the Sea-to-Sky region?**

We see that Outdoor adventurers venture into the backcountry without having done any research.

They may have seen a beautiful photo somewhere on social media and now just want to go there and experience the same beauty. Unfortunately often people will have no understanding what it may take to get to this location. This is where your research of the area should start....

Secondly: "this can not happen to me" seems an old false believe.

Unfortunately, it can happen to all of us, anytime, Preparedness can not avoid it but it s the key to reduce the potential severity of the incident and may change the outcome.

**4. Are there any local risks that visitors or beginners often underestimate, and how can people avoid them?**

As a person growing up in the mountains, we learn to understand certain risks travelling in the wonderful outdoors.

For a person new to outdoor recreation, this may be not at all be on their mind. It might be really hard for someone to understand that there are many risks in this beauty!

## Preparation and Physical Health

**5. What should people check or prepare before heading outdoors?**

Go with the principals of the 3 T's: Tripplan (planning phase) - Training (do I have the necessary training? e.g. avalanche training if I go into possible avalanche terrain, some first aid knowledge, navigation, .... )- The Essentials (what and how much do I need to carry?)

Please use information from the AdventureSmart page in terms of the 3 T's. https://www.adventuresmart.ca/programs/survive-outside/

Before you head out: do your planning: Route, Timing, Weather forecast, Exposure (e.g. Avalanche terrain), communication coverage, water resources, elevation gain and what does it mean, how do I get there (e.g. to the trailhead) and how to I get back, .....

**6. How can people judge whether an activity matches their fitness, skills, and experience? What are some warning signs that they should turn back?**

Excellent question!! Let's go with the example of a social media post of a beautiful lake in the backcountry!

You are intrigued and you want to go there! The post states it is an easy to moderate hike to the lake of about 6km. Elevation gain says 1200m with a few sections of a bit more technical terrain. The following comments from an experienced trail-runner state "yeah, that was easy" and the one below from a local hiker says "I've done this many times, there is one or two sections that are a bit trickier but doable). ..You stop reading after these comments and think, that this is "easy-peasy_. but it's actually not...

I would like people to explore reliable sources: e.g. check the BC parks page or the visitor centre information, speak with employees of the local climbing store, ...try to gain some neutral perspective of your endeavour.

I encountered once a group that explained how they had gotten themselves into the situation they were in.

They had done some research and had found out, that the trail description said 7kms with 900m elevation gain. The hikers only considered the 7kms, which they were used to walk in their area (on flat ground). The underestimation of the 900m elevation gain had gotten them into some massive trouble and required a SSAR response.

A few thoughts on the turning around: Turning around can be hard for people when they think they are almost there.... also for most people it is easier to go up then to go down.... before getting into more trouble, it is okay to call SAR to assist you.

Your warning sign should come during your planning phase and you should considers your "what if's" and set yourself your turn around points. e.g. If I don't get to xx by 14:00hrs I need to turn around to get back to the car before it get's dark.

You have to be very honest to yourself.

## What to Do in an Emergency

**7. What should someone do if they become lost or unsure of their route?**

Stop, Stay put and call 911! - Keep warm and dry!

**8. What should someone do if they or someone in their group gets injured and cannot continue?**

Stop, Assess, assist your friend and call 911, keep your group together!

**9. How can people get help without cell service, and what should they do while waiting for rescuers?**

It goes back to your planning phase from home! Did you consider a possible "no cell" service area and do you have another means of calling for help (emergency beacon, satellite phone, ...).

If you did not plan your back up, I hope you left at least a good trip-plan with a reliable friend/family member. This person will call 911 for you, if you do not show up or contact them at the time you agreed on.

While waiting for SAR, keep the group together and keep everyone warm by putting all the layers on that you have and wrap yourself in an emergency blanket.
