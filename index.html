Outdoor activities in this corridor

The Sea-to-Sky is beautiful and busy. Terrain can change from ocean to alpine in a short drive. Match the trip to your skills, not the photo.

Land

Hiking & scrambling

Chief, Garibaldi Lake, Black Tusk, Panorama Ridge, Joffre Lakes, and the Sea to Sky Gondola area see many rescues. Trails can be icy, snow-covered, or much longer than they look.

Snow

Ski, snowboard, snowmobile

This is avalanche country, including some popular hiking trails in shoulder season. Check avalanche.ca, carry transceiver, shovel, and probe, and know how to use them.

Water

Paddle, swim, bike, climb

Cold lakes, fast rivers, and Howe Sound can turn a short outing into an emergency. Wear a PFD on the water. Mountain biking and granite climbing need a plan, a partner, and a way to call for help.

What to do if…

In an emergency, use STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. Call 9-1-1 early. You will not be billed for a rescue in B.C.

If you are lost

  1. Stop moving. Stay together. Stay put if you told someone a route.
  2. Call or text 9-1-1. A 9-1-1 call can sometimes connect even without regular cell service.
  3. Give GPS coordinates from your phone’s compass or maps app.
  4. Use a whistle: three blasts, repeat. Make yourself visible.
  5. Shelter, extra layers, and food first. Do not bushwhack in the dark.

If someone is injured

  1. Make the scene safe. Do not create a second patient.
  2. Call 9-1-1 and ask for police. They dispatch Search and Rescue.
  3. Share location, number of people, injuries, and weather.
  4. Treat life-threatening bleeding, keep the person warm, and stay with them.
  5. Rescues can take hours. Prepare to wait.

If there is an avalanche

  1. If you are the survivor: shout, watch the burial site, switch your transceiver to search.
  2. Search, probe, shovel. Companions are the best chance of survival.
  3. Call 9-1-1 as soon as you can without stopping the search.
  4. Do not go into avalanche terrain without training and the full kit.

If you are in the water

  1. Wear the PFD. Cold B.C. water steals strength fast.
  2. Stay with the board or boat if it still floats.
  3. Call 9-1-1 from shore or a companion. For marine distress: VHF 16 or 1-800-567-5111.
  4. Do not swim rivers, log booms, or glacier-fed lakes alone.

If you meet wildlife

  • Make noise on the trail. Keep dogs leashed.
  • Never approach bears, cougars, or elk for a photo.
  • Back away slowly. Carry bear spray where it is legal and you know how to use it.
  • Report aggressive wildlife to the Conservation Officer Service: 1-877-952-7277.

If your phone is dying

  • Turn on Low Power Mode. Download offline maps before you go.
  • A battery pack is cheaper than a night out.
  • Carry a satellite messenger in places like Garibaldi, Sky Pilot, and Duffey Lake.
  • If you must call 9-1-1, say your location first.

The most common mistakes

B.C. Search and Rescue keeps seeing the same patterns: people get lost without a map, slip in poor shoes, or choose a trail harder than their skills. Half of the busiest hiking-rescue trails in the province are in this corridor.

01

No trip plan

Nobody knows where you went or when you are due back. Leave a written plan and a call-out time.

02

Trail harder than it looks

Rubble Creek, Black Tusk, the Chief, Habrich, and Joffre Lakes punish “we’ll just see.” Turn around early.

03

Late start, no light

Many rescues are people still on the trail after dark without a headlamp.

04

Phone as the only plan

Cell coverage dies in the trees, at the lake, and in the alpine. Phones also die.

05

Ignoring snow and ice

Popular summer trails hold snow late. Avalanche terrain is not only for skiers.

06

No PFD, cold water

Lakes that look calm are still cold. Wear the lifejacket. Do not mix alcohol and water.

07

Waiting to call

Calling 9-1-1 is free. Waiting until night, hypothermia, or no battery makes the rescue harder.

08

Forest service roads in winter

They are not maintained. Getting the car stuck can become a SAR call.

Emergency numbers

There is no special Sea-to-Sky rescue number. For backcountry emergencies, call 9-1-1 and ask for police. They activate the local volunteer SAR team.
